# Revenue Architecture Diagnostic Rubric

## Product contract

- Buyer: founder, CEO, or accountable GTM executive at a seed-to-Series B AI, data, or SaaS company.
- Trigger: one consequential go-to-market decision due within 60 days.
- Fee: confirmed in writing after fit.
- Delivery: five business days after payment, kickoff, and receipt of the complete evidence set.
- Included: 45-minute kickoff, review of up to eight artifacts, decision memo, 60-minute readout, and one 30-day outcome review.
- Excluded: campaign production, lead generation, sales outsourcing, implementation, broad organizational transformation, and simultaneous analysis of unrelated decisions.

## 1. Decision frame

Complete this before reviewing evidence.

| Field | Required definition |
| --- | --- |
| Decision | One choice leadership must make |
| Decision owner | Named person accountable for the call |
| Deadline | Date the decision must be made |
| Options | Two to four real alternatives |
| Constraints | Budget, timing, team, product, or market boundaries |
| Success condition | Observable result that would make the decision correct |
| Cost of error | Expected consequence of the wrong choice |
| Reversible elements | What can be tested before full commitment |

Reject or rescope the engagement when the decision cannot be expressed as one accountable choice.

## 2. Evidence register

Assign every artifact an evidence ID. Never cite an unsupported conclusion.

| Field | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Evidence ID | `E1`, `E2`, and so on |
| Source | File, system, interview, or public page |
| Owner | Person or function responsible for the source |
| Date | When the evidence was created or observed |
| Coverage | What decision question it can support |
| Limitations | Missing periods, selection bias, ambiguity, or stale data |
| Confidence | High, moderate, or low |

Evidence classes:

- **Supported fact:** directly present in a traceable source.
- **Reasonable inference:** the best explanation across multiple sources, with uncertainty visible.
- **Hypothesis:** plausible but not adequately tested.
- **Important unknown:** information that could materially change the decision.

## 3. Four-layer review

### Market architecture — Where can the company win?

Required questions:

- Which segment contains the strongest combination of urgency, fit, access, and economic value?
- What buying event causes the problem to become active?
- Who owns the problem, budget, and decision?
- Which wins, losses, and advanced opportunities share the same pattern?
- Is the operating ICP narrow enough to guide qualification and resource allocation?
- What segment or use case should the company explicitly decline for now?

Typical evidence:

- Customer interviews and call notes
- Pipeline by segment, source, stage, and outcome
- Win/loss records
- Usage or adoption patterns
- Competitive alternatives
- Market-entry assumptions

Material contradictions:

- Stated ICP does not match actual wins.
- Pipeline volume comes from segments that do not convert.
- The buyer, user, champion, and budget owner are conflated.
- Expansion-market logic is being used as the initial market.

### Narrative architecture — Why should the buyer care?

Required questions:

- Does the narrative begin with a costly buyer problem or with product capability?
- Can the economic and operational consequence be defended?
- Do website, demo, deck, sales calls, and customer proof tell the same story?
- Is differentiation based on a meaningful buyer choice?
- Does proof support the most important claim?
- Can a buyer explain the product and urgency after one interaction?

Typical evidence:

- Website and product pages
- Sales deck and demo narrative
- Call recordings or notes
- Objections and loss reasons
- Customer proof
- Competitive positioning

Material contradictions:

- Product category differs by team or channel.
- Claims are broader than available evidence.
- The narrative attracts interest but does not identify an accountable buyer.
- Sales proof demonstrates capability while the decision requires business justification.

### Revenue-motion architecture — How does demand become revenue?

Required questions:

- What are the actual stages from first signal through purchase and expansion?
- Where do qualified opportunities stall?
- Are qualification, value proof, and next-step criteria explicit?
- Is the proposed resource investment aimed at the current constraint?
- Do handoffs preserve the same customer problem and success definition?
- Which change can be tested before scaling headcount or spend?

Typical evidence:

- Funnel and CRM exports
- Stage conversion and time-in-stage
- Sales process and qualification criteria
- Launch and campaign outcomes
- Resource plan
- Onboarding and expansion records

Material contradictions:

- Leadership proposes adding volume when conversion is the constraint.
- Pipeline stages measure activity rather than buyer progress.
- Marketing, sales, and customer success use different qualification logic.
- The proposed hire assumes a motion that has not been repeated.

### Ecosystem architecture — Where can leverage compound?

Required questions:

- Which platforms, integrations, partners, or channels increase trust, access, or urgency?
- Does the company make an existing customer investment more valuable?
- Is the partner motion attached to a shared customer problem and operating workflow?
- Are partner-sourced opportunities materially different from direct opportunities?
- What must each party do for the motion to produce revenue?
- Is the ecosystem a source of leverage or a distraction from unresolved fundamentals?

Typical evidence:

- Integration usage
- Partner-sourced pipeline
- Co-sell and channel records
- Joint customer proof
- Platform dependencies
- Partner enablement and operating agreements

Material contradictions:

- Partnerships are measured by logos or activity rather than revenue progression.
- The company positions beside a platform rather than increasing its value.
- The partner and direct narratives target different problems.
- The motion depends on partner action that has no named owner or incentive.

## 4. Contradiction prioritization

For every contradiction, record:

1. Current assumption.
2. Observed evidence.
3. Revenue consequence.
4. Decision affected.
5. Confidence.
6. Evidence gap.

Prioritize using three internal factors:

- **Materiality:** How much revenue, time, or strategic flexibility is exposed?
- **Immediacy:** Will this affect the decision within 60 days?
- **Evidence strength:** How defensible is the contradiction?

Do not expose a composite maturity score. The output is the most consequential supported contradiction, not an average of unrelated practices.

## 5. Decision standard

The final recommendation must contain:

- The call in one sentence.
- The constraint or contradiction that makes it the best call.
- Evidence that supports it.
- Evidence that weakens it.
- Confidence level and why it is not higher.
- One reversible test when uncertainty remains.
- A 30-day action sequence with owners.
- Explicit actions leadership should not take yet.
- A measurable gate for revisiting the decision.

## Quality gate

The memo is not ready until:

- Every factual claim maps to an evidence ID.
- Inferences and unknowns are visibly labeled.
- The recommended action fits the stated constraints.
- The decision owner can accept or reject the recommendation.
- The 30-day plan contains owners and decision gates.
- No AI-generated statement is accepted without source review.
- Confidential client data is absent from reusable examples and marketing materials.
